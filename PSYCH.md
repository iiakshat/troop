GAME DESIGN RESEARCH BRIEF
PROJECT: ORIGINAL ARMY-GROWTH STRATEGY GAME
REFERENCE: TOP LORDS

==================================================
1. UNDERSTANDING THE REFERENCE GAME
==================================================

The central appeal is the fantasy of commanding an increasingly
powerful army.

In the gameplay observed, the player's army remains stationary
while enemies, numerical modifiers and obstacles approach.

The player must make quick decisions:

- Increase the size of their army.
- Multiply their current strength.
- Attack or weaken incoming enemies.
- Reduce incoming damage.
- Convert negative obstacles into positive advantages.
- Protect their army against upcoming threats.

The decisions look mathematically simple, but become interesting
because of compounding, percentages, operation order and the
consequences of choosing one option over another.

The player experiences:

1. Immediate power.
2. Visible progress.
3. A sense of competence.
4. Increasing confidence in their own decisions.
5. Satisfaction from defeating seemingly overwhelming enemies.

Top Lords also has a much larger kingdom-management, hero
recruitment and progression system. We should study its ability
to create progression without automatically copying its entire
structure.

Our game must have its own identity, mechanics, art direction
and progression.

==================================================
2. THE MOST IMPORTANT PSYCHOLOGICAL PRINCIPLES
==================================================

A. INSTANT POWER FANTASY

Give players meaningful power early.

The player should quickly experience:
- Their army becoming noticeably larger.
- Enemies being overwhelmed.
- A visible transformation in battlefield scale.
- Large, satisfying combat interactions.
- A clear improvement in their capabilities.

Do not make players spend the first 20 minutes earning the
right to experience the main fantasy.

Start with a small but functional army. Let players experience
substantial growth early.

B. SCAFFOLDED COMPETENCE

Make players feel intelligent by gradually teaching them the
game's systems.

The progression should be:

1. Show one simple decision.
2. Let the player make that decision.
3. Immediately demonstrate the consequence.
4. Introduce another mechanic.
5. Combine familiar mechanics.
6. Present situations requiring independent reasoning.

Initially, players should think:
"This is easy. I understand it."

Then:
"I know which option is better."

Later:
"I can predict what happens two decisions ahead."

Eventually:
"I can defeat an enemy much stronger than me because I
understand how this system works."

Do not maintain fake confidence through permanently trivial
challenges. Let the player's confidence become justified through
actual skill improvement.

C. CLEAR CAUSE AND EFFECT

Every decision should have a visible consequence.

Examples:
- Multiplying strength physically increases the army.
- Weakening an enemy visibly reduces its size or strength.
- Activating protection creates a visible shield.
- Converting a negative obstacle changes its appearance and
  turns it into a source of power.

The player must understand why something happened.

D. IMMEDIATE FEEDBACK

Do not delay the visual consequence of an important decision.

After a choice:
- Update the troop count.
- Show the operation being applied.
- Animate the army growing or changing.
- Display damage calculations.
- Show enemies losing strength.
- Make the eventual battle outcome understandable.

Use satisfying sound, animation, movement and impact.

The reward should be connected to the player's decision,
not an unrelated reward animation.

E. AGENCY AND AUTONOMY

The player must feel that their decisions determine the outcome.

Choices should have different consequences.

Avoid choices where one option is obviously superior every time.

Sometimes the player should prioritize:
- Army growth.
- Immediate damage.
- Protection.
- Future multipliers.
- Neutralizing an enemy's advantage.

Different situations should favour different decisions.

F. ANTICIPATION

Let players see incoming threats before they arrive.

Show enough information for them to think:

"Something dangerous is coming. I need to prepare."

The approaching battlefield should create tension.

The player should have time to recognise patterns and
consider the available options.

G. MASTERY

The strongest long-term motivation should be becoming better
at the actual game.

Introduce new mechanics and increasingly interesting combinations.

Players should remember:
"I solved that battle."

Not:
"I collected another meaningless reward."

==================================================
3. MATHEMATICAL PSYCHOLOGY: THE CORE GAMEPLAY
==================================================

The game's most important mechanic is rapid decision-making
using simple mathematics.

Players should learn to recognise:

- Addition.
- Subtraction.
- Multiplication.
- Division.
- Percentages.
- Compounding.
- Positive and negative modifiers.
- Damage reduction.
- Operation ordering.
- Risk versus reward.

Example:

Starting army = 100.

Option A: +50 soldiers.
Option B: ×2 army strength.

If the player chooses +50 and then ×2:

(100 + 50) × 2 = 300.

If they choose ×2 and then +50:

(100 × 2) + 50 = 250.

The two operations look simple, but their sequence matters.

Build puzzles around such interactions.

OTHER IMPORTANT EXAMPLES:

1. Enemy has 300 strength.
   Player has 100.
   Available choices:
   - Increase army by 100%.
   - Reduce enemy strength by 50%.
   - Activate a shield that absorbs 80 damage.

2. A negative obstacle reduces army size by 40%.
   The player can:
   - Accept the loss.
   - Spend a resource to neutralize it.
   - Convert it into a positive +40% modifier.

3. A weak army encounters a multiplier.
   Should the player recruit first or multiply first?

4. An enemy has armour that reduces incoming damage.
   Increasing troop numbers may not be as useful as removing
   the armour first.

5. An enemy regenerates health.
   The player may need to weaken it before increasing their
   own strength.

Every decision must be mathematically consistent.

Do not make every problem a simple comparison of the two
largest numbers.

==================================================
4. REWARD PSYCHOLOGY
==================================================

The game should deliver satisfaction through multiple layers.

LAYER 1: MICRO FEEDBACK

Occurs immediately after a decision.

Examples:
- Troops multiply.
- Army becomes visibly larger.
- An enemy loses strength.
- A negative gate transforms into a positive gate.
- A shield absorbs an incoming attack.

LAYER 2: DECISION SATISFACTION

The player realises they chose correctly.

For example:
They increased their army before using a multiplier and
ended up with significantly more soldiers.

LAYER 3: BATTLE PAYOFF

The army finally engages the approaching enemy.

Show:
- Large-scale battles.
- Units visibly fighting.
- Enemy formations breaking.
- Damage and casualties.
- The surviving army standing victorious.

LAYER 4: MASTERY PAYOFF

The player completes a difficult encounter by understanding
the mechanics.

This should be more satisfying than receiving random currency.

LAYER 5: LONG-TERM PROGRESSION

Give the player meaningful things to unlock.

Examples:
- New tactical operations.
- New unit types.
- New army formations.
- New defensive abilities.
- More complex enemy mechanics.
- New battlefield environments.

Unlocks should expand what the player can do, rather than
simply increasing numbers without adding gameplay depth.

==================================================
5. EARLY-GAME PSYCHOLOGY
==================================================

The first few minutes are extremely important.

The first experience should communicate:

"This game is easy to understand, satisfying to play,
and there is much more to discover."

Suggested progression:

FIRST ENCOUNTER:
Teach one simple army-growth decision.

SECOND ENCOUNTER:
Introduce multiplication.

THIRD ENCOUNTER:
Introduce enemy weakening.

FOURTH ENCOUNTER:
Introduce defensive mechanics.

FIFTH ENCOUNTER:
Combine two familiar operations.

SIXTH ENCOUNTER:
Introduce a negative obstacle that can be converted
into an advantage.

The player should gradually discover that understanding
the sequence of decisions matters more than blindly
choosing the largest number.

Give generous early progression, but do not make every
encounter effortless.

==================================================
6. DIFFICULTY AND CHALLENGE PSYCHOLOGY
==================================================

Use progressive complexity instead of simply increasing
enemy health.

Difficulty can increase through:

- More incoming decisions.
- Larger numerical values.
- Multiple modifiers.
- Multiplicative combinations.
- Enemies with special properties.
- Limited defensive resources.
- Conflicting strategic priorities.
- Decisions whose consequences occur later.
- Negative modifiers that can be converted.
- Situations where the obvious option is not the best option.

IMPORTANT:

Never make the game difficult merely by increasing enemy
numbers.

A difficult encounter should require a different way of thinking.

Introduce a mechanic, let the player understand it, and later
combine it with other mechanics.

The best difficulty curve makes players feel challenged,
not cheated.

==================================================
7. DARK PATTERNS FOUND IN FREE-TO-PLAY GAMES
==================================================

Understand these mechanisms and their effects.

They are included for competitive awareness, not as a
recommendation to exploit players.

A. ARTIFICIAL SCARCITY

Give players abundant resources initially, then sharply
restrict progression.

Why it works:
Players become accustomed to a particular speed of progress
and perceive the later slowdown as frustrating.

Risk:
Players may feel pressured to purchase resources.

Ethical alternative:
Use a deliberate progression curve. Never create artificial
friction just to sell relief.

B. PAY OR WAIT

Introduce long building timers or progress restrictions,
then sell speed-ups.

Why it works:
The player has already invested time and wants to continue.

Risk:
The game turns waiting into a monetisation mechanism.

Ethical alternative:
Do not make waiting necessary to access the core gameplay.

C. PAY OR GRIND

Require repetitive actions to obtain something, while
offering paid shortcuts.

Why it works:
Players exchange money to avoid unpleasant repetition.

Risk:
Progression becomes deliberately tedious.

Ethical alternative:
Make repeated gameplay enjoyable and provide meaningful
new challenges instead of artificial grinding.

D. FEAR OF MISSING OUT (FOMO)

Limited-time rewards, temporary events and expiring offers.

Why it works:
People may act to avoid losing an opportunity.

Risk:
Players feel pressured to return or spend at inconvenient
times.

Ethical alternative:
Use optional events without punishing players for absence.

E. DAILY LOGIN STREAKS

Reward consecutive daily logins.

Why it works:
Players develop a habitual return pattern.

Risk:
Missing one day can feel like losing accumulated progress.

Ethical alternative:
Offer flexible participation and cumulative rewards.

F. VARIABLE-RATIO REWARDS

Unpredictable rewards after repeated actions.

Why it works:
Uncertainty can increase anticipation and repeated behaviour.

Risk:
It can resemble gambling reinforcement, particularly when
random rewards are purchased.

Ethical alternative:
Keep rewards transparent and primarily skill-based.

G. LOOT BOXES

Offer random rewards, potentially requiring purchases.

Why it works:
The uncertainty of receiving a valuable item creates
anticipation.

Risk:
Players may repeatedly spend in pursuit of a rare outcome.

Ethical alternative:
Use direct unlocks and transparent reward systems.

H. NEAR-MISS DESIGN

Make players feel they almost succeeded.

Why it works:
Players may believe one more attempt will solve the problem.

Risk:
Deliberately manipulating outcomes can encourage excessive
retries or spending.

Ethical alternative:
Make failures understandable and teach players how to
improve.

I. SUNK-COST EFFECT

Players have already invested considerable time or money.

Why it works:
They may continue because abandoning the investment feels
like wasting it.

Risk:
Players remain engaged even when they no longer enjoy
the game.

Ethical alternative:
Make progression valuable without making players afraid
to leave.

J. LOSS AVERSION

Players dislike losing something they already own.

Why it works:
Protecting an existing army or resource can feel more urgent
than gaining an equivalent amount.

Ethical use:
Make defensive decisions meaningful.

Avoid:
Taking away permanent progress to pressure players into
spending.

K. PREMIUM CURRENCY OBSCURITY

Use currencies that make real-money prices difficult to
understand.

Why it works:
Players can lose track of the actual cost of transactions.

Risk:
Overspending becomes easier.

Ethical alternative:
Display clear real-money prices.

L. DIFFICULTY SPIKES

Suddenly increase difficulty after the player becomes
invested.

Why it works:
Frustration can make paid advantages appear attractive.

Risk:
Players feel that the game is deliberately making them lose.

Ethical alternative:
Difficulty should increase because new skills are required,
not because the player has reached a monetisation checkpoint.

M. SOCIAL PRESSURE

Use rankings, guilds, competitive events and other players'
progress.

Why it works:
Players compare themselves with others.

Risk:
Social pressure can push players into spending or playing
more than they intended.

Ethical alternative:
Reward skill and participation without creating pay-to-win
competition.

N. MISLEADING ADVERTISING

Show exciting gameplay that does not match the actual game.

Why it works:
The advertisement sells a more compelling experience than
the product delivers.

Risk:
Players lose trust and uninstall.

Ethical alternative:
Our advertisements must show actual gameplay.

O. MONETISING QUALITY OF LIFE

Charge for convenient features such as inventory space,
basic progression controls or normal gameplay comfort.

Why it works:
Players pay to remove inconveniences.

Risk:
The game intentionally makes ordinary activities frustrating.

Ethical alternative:
Make the baseline experience complete and enjoyable.

8. PSYCHOLOGICAL TECHNIQUES WE SHOULD ACTUALLY USE
==================================================

Prioritise these:

1. Immediate power fantasy.
2. Visible army growth.
3. Strong cause-and-effect feedback.
4. Scaffolding and progressive mastery.
5. Meaningful decisions.
6. Operation-order puzzles.
7. Anticipation of incoming threats.
8. Clear numerical feedback.
9. Satisfying battle resolution.
10. Gradually increasing complexity.
11. Meaningful unlocks.
12. Player agency.
13. Skill-based rewards.
14. Optional mastery challenges.
15. Clear progress indicators.
16. Strong visual and audio feedback.
17. Recognisable enemy patterns.
18. Increasingly sophisticated combinations.
19. Fair failure and understandable consequences.
20. A feeling of becoming a genuinely better commander.

Do not confuse satisfying game design with manipulation.

We want players to return because they enjoy solving
problems and commanding their armies.

==================================================
9. HOW OUR GAME MUST BE DIFFERENT
==================================================

We are inspired by the satisfying army-growth experience,
not attempting to recreate Top Lords.

Our defining gameplay identity should be:

A stationary army facing advancing threats, where the
player uses rapid arithmetic, tactical decisions and
operation ordering to control the outcome of a battle.

Potential differentiating mechanics:

A. OPERATION ORDERING
The order in which additions, multipliers and percentages
are applied changes the final result.

B. NEGATIVE-TO-POSITIVE CONVERSION
A dangerous obstacle can potentially become a source of
power through a clever choice.

C. ENEMY ATTRIBUTES
Different enemies have armour, shields, regeneration,
damage multipliers or other special properties.

D. TACTICAL INTERACTIONS
The player must decide whether to grow, attack, defend,
neutralise or exploit a particular threat.

E. COMPOUNDING
Earlier choices can make later choices substantially more
valuable.

F. DELAYED CONSEQUENCES
Some decisions should influence subsequent encounters,
requiring the player to plan ahead.

G. SIGNATURE ABILITIES
Allow players to unlock new ways of manipulating
battlefield mathematics rather than only obtaining
bigger numerical bonuses.

Our differentiation must come from gameplay, not merely
different character models or colours.

==================================================
10. VISUAL AND EMOTIONAL DESIGN
==================================================

The game should make army strength feel tangible.

Use:
- Hundreds of visible soldiers where performance allows.
- Distinct enemy formations.
- Large numerical indicators.
- Satisfying transformations.
- Strong impact animations.
- Visible damage and casualty effects.
- Clear positive and negative modifier colours.
- Distinct sounds for multiplication, recruitment,
  protection and conversion.
- Noticeable battlefield changes when decisions are made.

The army must feel like a force, not a number displayed
above a character.

Do not overload the screen with unnecessary buildings,
currencies, icons or menus.

The decision must remain the visual centre of attention.

==================================================
11. RETENTION WITHOUT MANIPULATION
==================================================

Give players genuine reasons to return.

Good retention mechanisms:

- New tactical mechanics.
- New enemy behaviour.
- New battlefield scenarios.
- Campaign challenges.
- Optional mastery objectives.
- Personal bests.
- Replayable encounters with different solutions.
- Meaningful progression.
- Periodic content updates.

Avoid:

- Forced daily logins.
- Artificial energy limits.
- Punishment for missing days.
- Artificial waiting.
- Pay-to-win advantages.
- Random paid progression.
- Endless repetitive grinding.

Do not optimise solely for screen time.

Optimise for:
- Enjoyment.
- Voluntary return.
- Successful learning.
- Fair challenge.
- Player satisfaction.
- Meaningful progression.

==================================================
12. MONETISATION PRINCIPLES
==================================================

The core game must remain enjoyable without spending.

Potential ethical monetisation:

- Cosmetic army skins.
- Alternative battlefield themes.
- Character customisation.
- Optional campaign expansions.
- One-time premium unlocks.
- Clearly priced optional content.

Avoid selling:
- Basic gameplay functionality.
- Necessary progression.
- Competitive superiority.
- Relief from intentionally frustrating mechanics.

Do not obscure prices or use confusing premium currencies.

==================================================
13. PRODUCT TESTING AND SUCCESS METRICS
==================================================

Measure whether the game is genuinely enjoyable.

Track:

- How quickly players understand the first decision.
- Completion of the first encounter.
- Whether players understand why they won or lost.
- Which choices they select.
- Whether players discover operation-order interactions.
- How often players retry after a failure.
- Completion of later encounters.
- Voluntary return.
- Player feedback about difficulty.
- Whether players describe decisions as interesting.

Test with people who have never seen the game.

Ask:
- Did you understand what was happening?
- Did your decisions matter?
- Did you feel clever?
- Was the challenge too easy or too difficult?
- Could you explain why you won?
- Did you want to try another encounter?

Do not mistake a high number of clicks or long session
duration for genuine enjoyment.

==================================================
14. FINAL DESIGN PHILOSOPHY
==================================================

The player should experience three feelings:

POWER:
"Look at the size of my army."

INTELLIGENCE:
"I figured out how to defeat an army much stronger than mine."

MASTERY:
"Give me a harder battle. I understand this system."

The central promise:

A smaller army can defeat an overwhelmingly larger army
when its commander understands the battlefield better.

Make players feel powerful because they are getting smarter,
not because the game is handing them meaningless numbers.

Create engagement through challenge, agency, progression
and satisfying consequences.

Make a game people genuinely enjoy playing, not a system
designed merely to keep them from putting their phones down.
